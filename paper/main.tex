\begin{abstract}
Dose equivalence calculations decide how radiotherapy schedules are compared,
compensated after an interruption, and summed in reirradiation. They are also
applied inconsistently: across identical clinical cases, inter-practitioner
agreement reaches only 25.8\,\% and nearly half of surveyed centres report no
usable tool. \texttt{LQL-Equiv} computes biologically effective dose,
equivalent dose in a reference fractionation, normal-tissue complication
probability, tumour control probability and radiation-induced cancer risk under
the linear-quadratic-linear model, with corrections for incomplete
inter-fraction repair, accelerated proliferation and treatment protraction,
over any number of successive courses separated by gaps. Version~3.0 is a
dependency-free Python library and a browser application that runs entirely on
the user's machine through WebAssembly: no installation, no licence fee, and no
computation on a server. It was regression-tested case by case against the 2014
MATLAB reference implementation over 4438 treatment schedules, agreeing on
69\,125 of 69\,131 compared values, its solver was checked against the original
exhaustive search over 10\,554 searches, and its equations are pinned
independently of both by closed-form tests. The complication and control
probabilities are point estimates on a single equivalent dose and have not been
validated against clinical outcome. It is intended for research and education
and is not a medical device.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and significance}

Radiotherapy schedules that differ in fraction size, fraction number or overall
time cannot be compared by adding up physical dose: 20~$\times$~3\,Gy and
30~$\times$~2\,Gy are both 60\,Gy and are not the same treatment. The comparison
is made through the biologically effective dose and the equivalent dose in a
reference fractionation, usually 2\,Gy per fraction (EQD2)
\cite{fowler1989,fowler2010,jones2001,bentzen2012,voyant2017}. These quantities govern three decisions
taken in every department: how to compensate an unscheduled interruption
\cite{rcr2019,jones2020,oshea2022}, whether a hypofractionated schedule is acceptable, and
how to accumulate dose in reirradiation \cite{appelt2026,hardcastle2024}.

The calculation is not applied consistently. In a structured workshop where
radiation oncologists, medical physicists and technologists applied the same
models to the same standardised cases, mean inter-practitioner agreement reached
25.8\,\% (Jaccard index), falling to 10\,\% for head-and-neck cases; proposed
dose-per-fraction adjustments for identical situations ranged from 1.8 to
2.5\,Gy, and nearly half of the surveyed centres reported that they could not
compute biologically equivalent schedules with the tools available to them
\cite{voyant2026standard,voyant2025}. That companion study is under revision at
the time of writing; its figures are quoted here as an indication of the size of
the problem and not as settled prevalence estimates, and the case for this
software does not rest on them. Multi-centre studies of reirradiation
reach the same conclusion from a different direction: intercentre variation in
radiobiological dose scaling introduces clinically relevant differences in
cumulative dose \cite{wahlstedt2026,hardcastle2024,stroom2025}. Where the
spinal cord is involved, that variation is not academic.

Part of that dispersion is not disagreement about radiobiology but about
vocabulary. Identical parameters yield substantially different reported EQD2
values depending on whether proliferation is applied to the evaluated schedule
only or symmetrically to the evaluated and the reference schedule. For one
head-and-neck course the two conventions differ by 15\,Gy
(Section~\ref{sec:examples}); both are defensible, and a value reported without
its convention cannot be reconciled with another. This software therefore states
its own convention as an equation rather than in prose.

Three modelling elements are needed and are routinely omitted. First, the
linear-quadratic model is widely held to overestimate cell kill at the fraction
sizes used in stereotactic treatments, although the evidence is tissue- and
endpoint-dependent and the point is not settled
\cite{astrahan2008,guckenberger2013,brown2014}; the linear-quadratic-linear
(LQL) form of Astrahan replaces the quadratic term by a straight line above a
transition dose. Second, overall treatment time matters: accelerated
proliferation after a kick-off time consumes dose every day, so an interruption
is not neutral \cite{withers1988,dale1989,dale2024}. Third, two fractions a day
require the incomplete-repair correction \cite{thames1985}. A tool that omits
any of the three cannot answer the questions above under these model
assumptions; a deliberately simpler model can of course answer a narrower
question.

Software implementing these models exists
\cite{jaikuna2017,tzikas2024,singh2018,yusoff2016,calvo2026}, and we published
one ourselves in 2014 \cite{voyant2014,lqlequiv2014soft}. That release was a
MATLAB GUIDE application distributed as a Windows executable requiring the
MATLAB Component Runtime. The barrier was practical rather than scientific: a
several-hundred-megabyte runtime, a single operating system, an interface in
French, and 3567 lines of source in which the entire calculation sat inside one
callback. It could not be inspected, cited by version, or run by a colleague in
a browser during a meeting.

\texttt{LQL-Equiv}~3.0 removes that barrier without discarding the validated
model. It is a reimplementation of the same calculation as a dependency-free
Python library and a browser application, validated case by case against the
original, with the provenance of every radiobiological parameter recorded.

\section{Software description}

\subsection{Architecture}

The package follows a \texttt{src} layout and separates three concerns. The
core, \texttt{src/lqlequiv/}, contains the model (\texttt{model.py}), the
calendar conversion (\texttt{schedule.py}) and the radiobiological library
(\texttt{tissues.py}, backed by \texttt{data/tissues.json}); it depends only on
the Python standard library, which keeps it auditable and makes it light enough
to ship to a browser. The interface, \texttt{app/streamlit\_app.py}, is a
Streamlit application. The delivery layer, \texttt{tools/build\_stlite.py},
embeds the sources into a single HTML page of about 112\,kB, which loads a
version-pinned stlite \cite{stlite} that in turn runs Streamlit on Pyodide
\cite{pyodide}; the page carries the model but not the runtime.
GitHub Actions rebuilds and publishes that page on every push; the whole cycle
takes about 25\,s.

The consequence is architectural rather than cosmetic (Fig.~\ref{fig:interface}).
No computation happens on a server: each visitor's browser downloads static
files and executes the model locally. The precise claim, since it matters when a
physicist types a real patient schedule, is this: the page embeds the model
source itself, fetches the version-pinned stlite and Pyodide runtime from a
public content-delivery network on first load, carries no analytics and submits
no form. The network therefore sees that the page was visited, and never what
was entered into it. A reader who must rely on that can confirm it from the
browser network panel; the delivery script is part of the repository.
Concurrent use is limited only by the number of visitors' own machines.

\subsection{Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

Notation is fixed once and used throughout. A course delivers $n$ fractions of
dose $d$ (Gy) and spans an overall time $T$ (days). The tissue is described by
its sensitivity ratio $\alpha/\beta$ (Gy), its linear coefficient $\alpha$
(Gy$^{-1}$), the transition dose $d_t = 2\,\alpha/\beta$ (Gy) above which the
response becomes linear, the slope of that linear tail $\gamma/\alpha$
(dimensionless, taken as 5), the repair half-time $T_{1/2}$ (h), the kick-off
time of accelerated proliferation $T_k$ (days) and the effective doubling time
$T_p$ (days).

The dose consumed per day by proliferation is
\begin{equation}
D_\mathrm{prol} \;=\; \frac{\ln 2}{\alpha\,T_p}
\qquad (\mathrm{Gy\,d^{-1}}),
\label{eq:dprol}
\end{equation}
and the incomplete-repair correction for $m$ fractions a day separated by
$\Delta t$ is that of Thames \cite{thames1985},
\begin{equation}
H_m \;=\; \frac{2\varphi}{m}\,\frac{1}{1-\varphi}
          \left[\,m - \frac{1-\varphi^{m}}{1-\varphi}\right],
\qquad
\varphi = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\ln 2\,\Delta t}{T_{1/2}}\right).
\label{eq:hm}
\end{equation}
The software delivers at most two fractions a day, six hours apart, and for
$m = 2$ the bracket is $2-(1+\varphi) = 1-\varphi$, so \eqref{eq:hm} collapses to
\begin{equation}
H_2 \;=\; \varphi \;=\; 2^{-\Delta t / T_{1/2}},
\qquad \Delta t = 6\,\mathrm{h},
\label{eq:h2}
\end{equation}
which is what the code evaluates; $H_1 = 0$ for one fraction a day, as
\eqref{eq:hm} gives. The general form is retained only to identify the
convention. Note that $m$ here counts fractions per day and is unrelated to the
Lyman parameter of equation~\eqref{eq:ntcp}. In \eqref{eq:dprol} and
\eqref{eq:h2} alike the 2014 release used the rounded constant 0.693 rather than
$\ln 2$; version 3.0 keeps it, since changing it would move every published
number by a few parts in ten thousand for no gain, and both tests pin it.

The biologically effective dose is linear-quadratic below the transition dose
and linear above it, both reduced by proliferation once $T$ exceeds $T_k$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BED}(d,n,T) =
\begin{cases}
\; n\,d\left[1 + \dfrac{(1+H_m)\,d}{\alpha/\beta}\right]
  - D_\mathrm{prol}\,(T-T_k)_{+}, & d < d_t,\\[2.2ex]
\; n\left[d_t\left(1 + \dfrac{d_t}{\alpha/\beta}\right)
  + \dfrac{\gamma}{\alpha}\,(d-d_t)\right]
  - D_\mathrm{prol}\,(T-T_k)_{+}, & d \geq d_t,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:bed}
\end{equation}
where $(x)_{+} = \max(x,0)$. The two branches are not independent choices. The
lower branch has slope $1 + 2d/(\alpha/\beta)$ in $d$, which at $d = d_t$ equals
$1 + 2d_t/(\alpha/\beta)$; requiring the linear tail to join it with a
continuous first derivative therefore fixes
\begin{equation}
\frac{\gamma}{\alpha} \;=\; 1 + \frac{2\,d_t}{\alpha/\beta} \;=\; 5
\qquad \text{when } d_t = 2\,\alpha/\beta .
\label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}
The single convention $d_t = 2\,\alpha/\beta$ thus determines the tail slope; the
value 5 is not a free parameter, and $\gamma/\alpha$ is dimensionless because
$\gamma$ and $\alpha$ are both in Gy$^{-1}$. Choosing a different transition dose
moves the slope with it. The convention is the one carried by the 2014 library
\cite{voyant2014}; Astrahan treats $d_t$ as fitted to the tissue
\cite{astrahan2008}, and a reader who prefers a fitted value should read
\eqref{eq:continuity} as the constraint any such choice must satisfy.

Equation~\eqref{eq:bed} is the whole model; everything else follows from one
equality. Let $d_r$ be the reference dose per fraction, conventionally 2\,Gy.
The equivalent dose is obtained by asking how many reference fractions produce
the same biological effect, that is by solving for $n_r$
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\mathrm{BED}\big(d_r,\,n_r,\,T(n_r)\big)
       \;=\;
       \mathrm{BED}\big(d,\,n,\,T(n)\big)\;}
\label{eq:core}
\end{equation}
and then reading
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{EQD}_{d_r} \;=\; n_r\,d_r .
\label{eq:eqd}
\end{equation}

Equations \eqref{eq:bed}--\eqref{eq:eqd} are what \texttt{LQL-Equiv}~3.0 solves.
They are not what the 2014 application computed for the organ at risk. That
release reported $n_r d_r - (T - T_r)\,D_\mathrm{prol}$, and the difference
matters: for a rectum receiving 20~$\times$~3\,Gy it reported 82.69\,Gy where
\eqref{eq:eqd} gives 75.25\,Gy. The reported value does not satisfy the equality
it is derived from. Delivering 82.69\,Gy at the reference fraction size returns a
biologically effective dose of 107.74\,Gy against the schedule's own 97.75\,Gy,
because proliferation is subtracted once inside the search and once again
afterwards. The tumour branch of the same routine carries no such term.
\texttt{docs/COMPARISON-2014.md} gives the arithmetic, the criterion that separates a
convention from an inconsistency, and the size of the difference over 7850
schedules. \texttt{Options.legacy\_2014()} reproduces the 2014 behaviour for
anyone recomputing a result published before 2026, and is what the validation of
Section~\ref{sec:verification} measures; nothing else uses it.

Two points about \eqref{eq:core} are easy to miss and account for most of the
disagreement between implementations. First, the reference schedule on the left
carries its own overall time $T(n_r)$ and therefore its own proliferation loss;
definitions that correct only the right-hand side answer a different question
and return a different number. Second, $T$ is not $n$: treatment runs five days a week, and $m$ fractions a day
occupy $\lceil n/m \rceil$ treatment sessions. Writing $s_j$ for the sessions of
course $j$ and $g_j$ for the sessions missed before it, one absolute calendar
serves both tissues and both sides of \eqref{eq:core},
\begin{equation}
T(x) \;=\; \frac{7}{5}\,x,
\qquad
x_j \;=\; \sum_{i \leq j}\left(\left\lceil \frac{n_i}{m} \right\rceil + g_i\right),
\label{eq:time}
\end{equation}
the course occupying the increment $T(x_j) - T(x_{j-1})$. Ten fractions delivered
twice a day therefore occupy five sessions, not ten. Because $g$ is counted in
sessions rather than in calendar days, weekends inside an interruption are
supplied by the $7/5$ rate itself.

The rate is used in place of an integer weekend staircase, and the choice is
forced. A staircase is not additive, $\Theta(40) = 54$ against
$2\,\Theta(20) = 52$, so splitting a course into two would manufacture two days
of proliferation; and $n_r$ is a real number solved from an equality, which a
step function makes neither invertible nor additive. The rate $7/5$ is the exact
long-run slope of that staircase. The staircase remains available as the nominal
calendar a department reads off a wall, and is reported as such. Neither model
knows about public holidays or a course starting mid-week.

For several successive courses indexed by $j$, the reference schedule is not
restarted at each course. Writing $\tau_j$ for the elapsed reference time before
course $j$, with $\tau_1 = 0$, the software solves
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BED}\big(d_r,\,n_{r,j},\,\tau_j\big)
\;=\; \mathrm{BED}\big(d_j,\,n_j,\,T(x_j) - T(x_{j-1})\big),
\qquad
\tau_{j+1} = \tau_j + \frac{7}{5}\,n_{r,j},
\label{eq:multi}
\end{equation}
each course being matched against the segment of one continuous reference
schedule that follows the segments already spent, both sides carrying the
proliferation accumulated before them. Proliferation is therefore subtracted once
over the total elapsed time and never twice, and the equivalent doses add,
$\mathrm{EQD} = \sum_j n_{r,j}\,d_r$.

Additivity is a property to be proved, not assumed: \eqref{eq:core} is piecewise
linear in $n_r$ with a kink at $T_k$, and the inverse of a nonlinear map is not
additive in general. It holds here because both calendars are linear in the
session count, so the reference losses telescope,
$\sum_j [\,G(\tau_{j+1}) - G(\tau_j)\,] = G(\tau_{J+1})$ with
$\tau_{J+1} = \tfrac{7}{5}\sum_j n_{r,j}$, and the total depends on the courses
only through that sum. \texttt{tests/test\_analytic.py} pins it at a fraction
size away from $d_r$, where the identity is not already trivial.

The two toxicity endpoints follow from the cumulative equivalent dose. The
normal-tissue complication probability uses the Lyman probit \cite{lyman1985},
with $D_{50}$ the dose giving a 50\,\% complication rate (Gy) and $m$ the
dimensionless dispersion parameter, which is inversely proportional to the
steepness of the response and is not itself a slope:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NTCP} = \frac{1}{2}\left[1 + \operatorname{erf}
  \left(\frac{u}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right],
\qquad u = \frac{\mathrm{EQD} - D_{50}}{m\,D_{50}} .
\label{eq:ntcp}
\end{equation}
The tumour control probability uses a logistic sigmoid through the dose giving
50\,\% control, $\mathrm{TCD}_{50}$ (Gy), with normalised slope $\gamma_{50}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{TCP} = \left[1 +
  \left(\frac{\mathrm{TCD}_{50}}{\mathrm{EQD}}\right)^{4\gamma_{50}}
  \right]^{-1}.
\label{eq:tcp}
\end{equation}
The interface also offers the Poisson form
$\mathrm{TCP} = 2^{-\exp[e\,\gamma_{50}(1 - \mathrm{EQD}/\mathrm{TCD}_{50})]}$,
which shares the same two parameters and differs in the tails; the choice is
reported with the value. Finally, the radiation-induced cancer risk follows a
linear-exponential form with $P$ in Gy$^{-1}$ and $\alpha_2$ in Gy$^{-1}$:
\begin{equation}
K = P\,\mathrm{EQD}\,e^{-\alpha_2 \mathrm{EQD}} ,
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
$K$ being a dimensionless excess incidence on the scale of the fitted $P$, and
reported as such rather than as an absolute lifetime risk.

\subsection{Functionalities}

For a chosen organ at risk, a chosen target volume and a sequence of successive
courses each with its own fraction size, fraction number and preceding gap, the
software computes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the biologically effective dose, linear-quadratic below the transition
        dose $d_t = 2\,\alpha/\beta$ and linear above it \cite{astrahan2008};
  \item the loss to proliferation, accumulated across courses and gaps, under the
        two models the tissues take: switched on at the kick-off time $T_k$ for
        the target \cite{dale1989,withers1988}, and accruing from the first day
        for the organ at risk \cite{vandyk1989};
  \item the incomplete-repair correction for two fractions a day, six hours
        apart \cite{thames1985};
  \item the equivalent dose in any reference fractionation, for the organ at
        risk and for the target;
  \item the normal-tissue complication probability, Lyman probit
        \cite{lyman1985,burman1991};
  \item the tumour control probability, logistic or Poisson sigmoid in
        $\gamma_{50}$;
  \item the radiation-induced cancer risk under a linear-exponential model.
\end{itemize}

The shipped library holds 34 organs at risk and 20 target sites. Nineteen of the
latter are transcribed from the 2014 release; the twentieth was added in 3.0, a
standard tumour with proliferation switched off, which isolates the effect of
fractionation from the effect of overall time. The interface offers sliders or
typed entry, an isoeffect sweep over fraction number or fraction size, a
therapeutic window in both directions, scenario comparison with CSV and JSON
export, and an $\alpha/\beta$ sensitivity band.

That last feature follows the systematic review of published $\alpha/\beta$
values, which recommends exploring a range rather than trusting a point estimate
\cite{vanleeuwen2018}. Because $\alpha/\beta$ also sets the transition dose
$d_t$, the response is not monotonic in it, so the envelope is sampled at nine
points rather than at its two ends.

\subsection{Historical multicentre evaluation}

The model was evaluated across centres when it was first published
\cite{voyant2014}: eight clinical calculators in seven French radiotherapy
centres converted the same standard schedules, and did not agree. Relative
standard deviations reached 17.7\,\% for the spinal cord and 29.6\,\% for a
prostate metastasis on a single 8\,Gy fraction, stayed above 5\,\% on
conventional 10~$\times$~3\,Gy schedules, and most centres could not compute a
schedule containing a gap at all; the 2014 outputs were also compared against
TDF~Plan \cite{tdfplan} schedule by schedule. That comparison against external
calculators was not repeated for version 3.0.

The survey is twelve years old and its finding has not aged
\cite{voyant2026standard,wahlstedt2026,hardcastle2024}, but it bears on the 2014
application and is context, not evidence for the present release. What follows
verifies that version~3.0 computes what the evaluated implementation computed,
which is a claim about fidelity, and then checks the equations against closed
forms, which is a claim about correctness. Neither is a clinical validation.

\subsection{Numerical verification of version 3.0}
\label{sec:verification}

The 2014 application still runs under MATLAB R2025b and can be driven without a
display. \texttt{tools/matlab/sweep.m} opens the figure invisibly, sets the
input fields, invokes the callbacks in the order the interface would, and reads
the output fields back, over 4438 treatment schedules: every organ/target pair
on a reference schedule, a wide fractionation grid from 1.2 to 20\,Gy per
fraction and 1 to 100 fractions with gaps and two-fractions-a-day schedules, and
600 randomised multi-course plans. The captured outputs form
\texttt{tests/data/golden.jsonl}, which the test-suite replays.

MATLAB writes its interface fields with six significant digits, which sets the
resolution at which the two implementations can be compared at all. At that
resolution they agree on all but six values, belonging to two cases in which the
exact root falls precisely halfway between two points of the original search
grid, so that the 2014 answer is decided by floating-point noise at the
fifteenth decimal. The consequence is a shift of one grid step in $n_r$, worth
up to 0.04\,Gy on the equivalent dose, which is the largest deviation in
Table~\ref{tab:validation}.

Both implementations are deterministic, so there is no sampling process and no
population to infer to. Significance tests and confidence intervals are
therefore not reported here: a $p$-value on a fixed corpus of paired
computations measures nothing, and a mean signed difference can average away
opposite errors of clinical size. What is reported instead is the whole
distribution of the absolute difference against a tolerance fixed in advance
(Fig.~\ref{fig:agreement}, Table~\ref{tab:validation}). The tolerance is
$\delta = 0.05$\,Gy, chosen because the smallest dose increment any of these
decisions turns on is of the order of a tenth of a gray, and every one of the
34\,418 paired values clears it, the largest deviation being 0.04\,Gy.

A second check does not depend on the MATLAB outputs. The original locates the
equivalent fraction count by evaluating an objective at 20\,001 grid points and
taking the minimum; version 3.0 solves the objective in closed form and scores
only the grid points around each root. That shortcut is legitimate only if it
lands on the same point every time, which is not obvious because proliferation
switching on at $T_k$ makes the tumour objective piecewise linear, so it can
approach the target twice and its best grid point can lie next to the kink
rather than next to a root. \texttt{tools/verify\_grid\_equivalence.py} replays
the full grid for every case: 10\,554 searches, no disagreement.

Neither check can establish that the equations are the intended ones, because
both are anchored to the 2014 code. That distinction is not academic: it is
exactly how the three legacy conventions of Section~\ref{sec:formulation} escaped
notice for twelve years, since a test that asks whether two implementations agree
cannot ask whether either is right. A third layer therefore evaluates the
formulation against values obtained by hand from
\eqref{eq:dprol}--\eqref{eq:multi}, with no reference implementation in the loop.
\texttt{tests/test\_analytic.py} pins that $\mathrm{EQD}_{d_r} = n d$ when
$d = d_r$, that \eqref{eq:bed} is continuous at $d_t$ with the slope
\eqref{eq:continuity} requires, that $H_2$ equals $\varphi$ of \eqref{eq:h2} to
machine precision, and that the proliferation loss vanishes for $T \leq T_k$.
Four of the sixteen checks target the corrected convention specifically, and each
of them fails under the legacy one: that the reported dose is $n_r d_r$ exactly,
that the organ-at-risk result moves when $T_k$ moves, that both tissues see two
fractions a day, and that splitting a course into two, three or four segments
leaves the total unchanged. The last is asserted at a fraction size away from
$d_r$: at $d = d_r$ the identity holds trivially, and an earlier version of this
test passed while the property was broken.

The reimplementation surfaced four further defects in the 2014 release, all now
documented and all reachable through \texttt{Options.legacy\_2014()} so that
historical results remain obtainable. Equivalent doses beyond 100 reference
fractions were reported at the search bound, silently, and stopped responding to
added dose. Nine parameters were displayed differently from the values used in
the computation, across seven tissues. The calendar model contradicts itself past
86 fractions, where two copies of the same weekend staircase diverge by up to a
day. Complication probability was reported as 100\,\% for the five tissues that
carry no Lyman parameters, through a division by zero; it is now reported as
unavailable. The fifth, and the only one that changes an answer a department
would read, is the organ-at-risk equivalent dose documented in
\texttt{docs/COMPARISON-2014.md}.

\paragraph{Clinical plausibility.} None of the above says whether the model
matches clinical experience, and no test here can. What can be asked is whether
schedules that randomised trials compared directly come out where the trials
placed them. Against 78\,Gy in 39 fractions, the software puts the
ultra-hypofractionated arm of HYPO-RT-PC at 77.94\,Gy \cite{widmark2019hypo},
and it orders the two hypofractionated arms of CHHiP as the trial did, the one
that met non-inferiority above the one that did not \cite{dearnaley2016chhip}.
It also disagrees: 60\,Gy in 20 fractions comes out 5.5\,Gy below the
conventional arm of PROFIT, which demonstrated non-inferiority without hormone
therapy \cite{catton2017profit}. That gap closes under
$\alpha/\beta = 1.5$\,Gy, near the pooled clinical estimate of 1.4\,Gy
\cite{miralbell2012alphabeta}, which is the clearest evidence that the tabulated
3.1\,Gy is not universal. On the organ side the tabulated Lyman parameters fail
outright: 50\,Gy in 25 fractions to the spinal cord returns a complication
probability of 5.1\,\% where QUANTEC puts the risk near 0.2\,\%
\cite{kirkpatrick2010quantec}, a discrepancy also reported clinically
\cite{daly2012lkb}. \texttt{docs/CLINICAL-PLAUSIBILITY.md} works through all of
these, with PACE-B \cite{vanas2024paceb} and the stereotactic cord constraint of
HyTEC \cite{sahgal2021hytec}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The software is a model comparison tool and not a treatment planning system: it
knows nothing of dose distribution, volume effects or plan constraints, and
voxel-level accumulation \cite{singh2018,calvo2026} is outside its scope. Two
consequences follow for the toxicity endpoints and should be stated plainly.
Equation~\eqref{eq:ntcp} is a point estimate on a single equivalent dose,
whereas the Lyman formalism is defined on a dose-volume histogram through an
equivalent uniform dose \cite{niemierko1997} and is established near
2\,Gy per fraction; away from that fraction size, and for a serial organ where
the relevant quantity is a near-maximum rather than a mean, it should be read as
indicative. Equation~\eqref{eq:tcp} uses $\gamma_{50}$ and
$\mathrm{TCD}_{50}$ values that the 2014 library carried but never used, so the
tumour control probability is newly exposed and, unlike the equivalent dose, has
not itself been validated against clinical outcome. The
radiobiological library is the one published in 2014; its Lyman parameters fall
in the range of the Emami/Burman fits \cite{emami1991,burman1991}, which is
their probable but unverified origin, and the provenance of every value is
recorded in \texttt{docs/PARAMETERS.md}. Model parameters carry uncertainty
larger than many of the differences the software can display, which is why the
$\alpha/\beta$ band exists and why the therapeutic window declines to name a
best schedule when the uncertainty bars overlap.

It is intended for research and education and is not a medical device. Stated
operationally rather than generically, its outputs must not be used to choose or
modify a prescription, to compute the compensation for a real interruption, to
sum dose in a reirradiation decision, to accept or reject a treatment plan, to
support a consent discussion, or to predict complication or control probability
for an individual patient. What it is for is comparing schedules under a stated
model, seeing how far that comparison moves when the assumptions move, and
teaching why.

\section{Illustrative examples}
\label{sec:examples}

The library is used in a few lines. The example below reproduces a prostate
schedule of 39~$\times$~2\,Gy with the rectum as organ at risk:

\begin{verbatim}
from lqlequiv import Course, Prescription, compute, load_library

lib = load_library()
plan = Prescription(courses=(Course(2.0, 39),),
                    reference_dose=2.0)
res = compute(lib.organ("Rectum"),
              lib.tumour_site("Prostate"), plan)

res.eqd_oar_total   # 78.00 Gy EQD2 to the rectum
res.ntcp_percent    # complication probability, proctitis
res.tcp_percent     # tumour control probability
\end{verbatim}

\paragraph{Cost of an interruption.} A course of 25~$\times$~2\,Gy delivered to
the standard tumour reaches 50.00\,Gy EQD2. Ten missed treatment sessions, which
equation~\eqref{eq:time} turns into fourteen additional calendar days, bring it
to 37.48\,Gy: the interruption costs 12.5\,Gy EQD2, more than six 2\,Gy
fractions. Against the added non-proliferating tumour the same interruption
costs nothing, which separates the time effect from the fractionation effect.
This is the quantity that compensation guidance asks a department to estimate
\cite{rcr2019,jones2020,dale2024}, and the one the workshop found practitioners
estimating inconsistently \cite{voyant2026standard}.

\paragraph{Two definitions of the same word.} For 35~$\times$~2\,Gy in a
head-and-neck target ($\alpha/\beta = 10$\,Gy, $\alpha = 0.3$\,Gy$^{-1}$,
$T_k = 21$\,d, $T_p = 4$\,d) the companion standardisation study reports a
time-corrected EQD2 of 54.60\,Gy \cite{voyant2026standard}; this software
returns 70.00\,Gy from the same $D_\mathrm{prol} = 0.5775$\,Gy\,d$^{-1}$ given by
equation~\eqref{eq:dprol}. The study divides the schedule's own BED by
$1+d_r/(\alpha/\beta)$; equation~\eqref{eq:core} matches it against a reference
schedule that is itself protracted, so at $d = d_r$ the two proliferation losses
cancel and the equivalent dose returns the physical dose. The first measures
effect delivered, the second schedule equivalence. Neither is wrong, and the
interface names which one it reports.

\paragraph{How far the model form moves the answer.} A department does not
compare schedules in the abstract. It fixes the effect on the target and asks
what the change costs the organ. Figure~\ref{fig:fractionation} runs that
calculation for a prostate treatment: the fraction count is swept over the
integers and the fraction size solved so that the target receives 78\,Gy EQD2,
so every point is a deliverable schedule, the control estimate is constant by
construction, and only the organ moves. The bands are a log-normal $\pm$30\,\%
uncertainty on the organ $\alpha/\beta$ (2000 draws, seed and parameterisation
in \texttt{paper/figures.py}), that spread being the order of the dispersion
between clinical series \cite{vanleeuwen2018,brenner2003}.

Read at five fractions, the three assumptions disagree on the sign of the
effect. Under the pure linear-quadratic form with $\alpha/\beta = 1.5$\,Gy for
the target -- the low ratio pooled prostate series support, and the usual
argument for stereotactic treatment -- 5~$\times$~6.65\,Gy is iso-effective on
the target and lowers the organ dose to 62.51\,Gy EQD2 against 78.00\,Gy for
39~$\times$~2\,Gy. The same ratio under the linear-quadratic-linear form requires
5~$\times$~8.42\,Gy and returns 95.19\,Gy, above the conventional schedule
instead of below it. The transition dose is $2\,\alpha/\beta$, so a low ratio
places it at 3\,Gy, beneath every fraction size at issue, and the linear tail
removes the target's advantage before it can be spent. The tabulated 3.1\,Gy sits
between them, at 5~$\times$~7.55\,Gy and 78.61\,Gy, which is the conventional
schedule to within a gray.

This is a statement about three models and not about patients; the trial evidence
is the arbiter of the clinical question. HYPO-RT-PC randomised 42.7\,Gy in 7
fractions against 78.0\,Gy in 39 and found non-inferior failure-free survival,
with early toxicity worse in the hypofractionated arm and late toxicity
comparable \cite{widmark2019hypo}. PACE-B randomised 36.25\,Gy in 5 fractions and
also found non-inferior biochemical control, but with more grade~2 or worse
genitourinary toxicity at five years, 26.9\,\% against 18.3\,\%
\cite{vanas2024paceb}; the two trials therefore agree on control and not on toxicity.
Under the tabulated parameters the software puts the HYPO-RT-PC regimen at
7~$\times$~6.10\,Gy, that is 42.7\,Gy, and returns 77.60\,Gy EQD2 to the organ
against 78.00\,Gy for the conventional arm. That is a numerical coincidence of
prescribed dose within one model and one parameter set, not a reproduction of the
trial's endpoints; the software's complication percentages come from a point dose
with legacy parameters and no volume information, and are not comparable with
measured toxicity. What the figure supports is narrower and is the reason the tool
exists: the choice between LQ and LQL is not a detail of formalism but the largest
single term in the answer, and a calculator that hides which one it used cannot be
reconciled with another.

\paragraph{Where the linear tail changes the answer.} Holding total physical
dose at 60\,Gy and varying fraction size, with proliferation switched off on
both tissues so that the comparison isolates fractionation, the two forms agree
exactly up to the transition dose ($d_t = 4$\,Gy for the spinal cord) and
separate above it: the linear-quadratic form returns 9.1\,\% more equivalent dose
at 6\,Gy per fraction, 42.9\,\% more at 10\,Gy and 90.3\,\% more at 15\,Gy
(Fig.~\ref{fig:lql}a). Across the 34 organs of the library the excess at 12\,Gy
per fraction has a median of 25.0\,\% and an interquartile range of
10.8--46.0\,\%, reaching 233.7\,\% for the tissues of lowest transition dose and
vanishing for those whose transition lies above the fraction size
(Fig.~\ref{fig:lql}b). In the stereotactic range the choice of formalism is no
longer a detail \cite{astrahan2008,guckenberger2013,brown2014}.

\section{Impact}

The first impact is access. The calculation is now reachable from a single URL,
on any modern browser, with no installation, no licence and no operating-system
constraint. In a field where nearly half of surveyed centres report no usable
tool \cite{voyant2026standard}, removing the installation barrier is not a
convenience: it is the difference between a model being applied and being
skipped.

The second is traceability. Every parameter's provenance is recorded, the
defects of the previous release are documented rather than silently corrected,
and the historical behaviour remains reachable through a switch so that results
computed in 2014 can still be reproduced. The validation dataset ships with the
code, so a reader can rerun it. This is what allows a departmental protocol to
name the exact version used, by DOI \cite{lqlequivweb}.

The third is reproducibility across centres. The multi-centre studies cited
above attribute part of the variation in cumulative dose to differences in how
the same model is applied \cite{wahlstedt2026,hardcastle2024,appelt2026}. A
version-pinned, openly inspectable implementation that every participant can run
in a browser removes one term from that variance. It does not remove the
disagreement over parameters, which is a scientific question
\cite{vanleeuwen2018}, but it separates it from the disagreement over
arithmetic, which is not.

The fourth is teaching. The isoeffect curves, the therapeutic window in both
directions and the non-proliferating reference tumour make visible effects that
are otherwise asserted: that a gap costs dose, that hypofractionation can gain
or lose depending on the geometry, and that the differences under discussion are
frequently smaller than the uncertainty of the parameters that produce them.

\section{Conclusions}

\texttt{LQL-Equiv}~3.0 makes a linear-quadratic-linear dose equivalence
calculation available without installation, with its assumptions stated as
equations, those equations pinned by closed-form tests, and its agreement with
the published reference implementation quantified over 4438 schedules. The models it implements are not
new; what it changes is that they can now be run identically, and inspected, by
everyone who needs them. Work in progress covers an updated parameter set drawn
from the post-2014 literature, carrying a citation per value, and reporting of
tolerance doses alongside the computed equivalent dose.
